CHAPTER 9: YEAR-1 CORE, PROJECT MANAGEMENT & PROCESS

How a small team actually gets it done

Good software is also a question of how you work: break the work down, make progress visible, name the risks, share the load, and improve every cycle. Process is what keeps a four-person team from quietly falling apart the week before the demo.

intro2se-ch9-project-management.fig / Planning & estimation
IDEA 1: SMALL TASKS, ROUGH ESTIMATES

Break work down until each piece is easy to judge

Break it down

One FR → a few tasks

“Add reminders” becomes: model, due-date logic, list UI, test. Small tasks are easy to estimate, assign and finish.

Estimate roughly

Small / Medium / Large

You can't predict hours precisely — don't pretend to. A rough size is enough to spot the task that's secretly huge.

In plain terms

A task you can't estimate is a task you don't understand yet — split it until you do. Progress becomes visible when work is many small done/not-done pieces instead of one big “nearly there.”

intro2se-ch9-project-management.fig / Estimation
IDEA 1b: ESTIMATION (COHN; SOMMERVILLE CH23)

Size with story points, not false-precise hours

Relative size

Story points

Compare tasks to each other (1, 2, 3, 5, 8…) instead of guessing exact hours no one can predict.

Together

Planning poker

Everyone estimates at once, then the outliers explain — that conversation is where hidden complexity surfaces.

Forecast

Velocity

Points finished per sprint. After a sprint or two you can forecast what the team can realistically take on.

In plain terms

A task you can't size is one you don't understand yet — split it (Idea 1). Relative sizing is faster and, across many tasks, more honest than fake-precise hours.

Sources: Cohn (2005) Agile Estimating and Planning; Sommerville 9e, Ch23 Project planning.

intro2se-ch9-project-management.fig / Concept check 4
CONCEPT CHECK 4: BREAK DOWN AND FORECAST

Split the FR, then forecast with velocity

FR: "A student can book a tutoring room"

Call out tasks, then vote S / M / L for each.

  • Pick date and time page
  • Check whether the room is free
  • Save the booking
  • Confirm and cancel page
  • Tests

Forecast

Sprint 1 finished 18 points, sprint 2 finished 22. 60 points remain. About how many more sprints?

Velocity = (18 + 22) / 2 = 20 points per sprint, so 60 / 20 = about 3 sprints.

Why story points, not hours?

Comparing sizes is easier than guessing exact hours, and across many tasks it comes out more accurate.

intro2se-ch9-project-management.fig / Concept check 4, explained: points
CONCEPT CHECK 4, EXPLAINED (2 OF 3): WHERE THE POINTS COME FROM

Points = size compared with a reference task, not hours

1

Pick a reference task

A small task everyone understands. "Save the booking" = 1 point.

2

Compare every other task with it

About the same = same points. About twice = 2. Bigger = the next number up the scale: 1, 2, 3, 5, 8, 13.

3

Size = effort + complexity + uncertainty

A task you are unsure about gets more points, even if the code looks short.

4

Vote together (planning poker)

Everyone shows a card at once. Highest and lowest explain, then vote again. 13 or more? Split the task.

Task (from the last slide)S/M/LPoints

Why the gaps grow (1, 2, 3, 5, 8)

Bigger tasks are less certain, so we stop pretending to tell 6 from 7. Points belong to one team: never compare one team's points with another's.

intro2se-ch9-project-management.fig / Concept check 4, explained: forecast
CONCEPT CHECK 4, EXPLAINED (3 OF 3): HOW TO FORECAST

Velocity: average what you actually finished, then divide

1

Velocity = average points Done per sprint

(18 + 22) / 2 = 20 points per sprint. Count only Done work; "nearly done" counts as 0.

2

Sprints needed = points left / velocity

60 / 20 = about 3 sprints.

3

Say "about", not "exactly"

Velocity moves (18, then 22). A forecast is a planning tool, not a promise.

Common traps

60 / 22 = 2.7, "so 3": right number, wrong reasoning (best sprint only, too optimistic).
60 / 18 = 3.3: worst sprint only, too cautious. Use the average.

Why story points, not hours?

People compare sizes ("about twice as big") better than they guess hours. Across many tasks, over- and under-estimates cancel out. A 3-point task stays 3 points whoever does it; hours depend on the person.

Only Done counts, which is why the board limits Doing (next slide). Sources: Cohn (2005); Sommerville 9e, Ch23.

intro2se-ch9-project-management.fig / Sprints & boards
IDEA 2: SHORT CYCLES, A VISIBLE BOARD

Work in sprints; track it on To-do / Doing / Done

A sprint = a short fixed cycle (e.g. 1 week). The board shows, at a glance, who's doing what
Why limit “Doing”

Too many things half-done = nothing finished. Finish before you start the next — that's how a small team ships.

intro2se-ch9-project-management.fig / Burndown chart
IDEA 2c: TRACK A SPRINT WITH A BURNDOWN

Work remaining should fall toward zero

Above the ideal line = behind schedule; below = ahead. The point is to see it early and adjust

A simple, honest status everyone can read at a glance — Scrum practice (Cohn, 2005).

In plain terms

It's a fuel gauge for your work. The dotted line is “where you should be by now”; if the real line stays above it, you'll run out of time — and you can see that early enough to react.

intro2se-ch9-project-management.fig / Concept check 5, explained: classify
CONCEPT CHECK 5, EXPLAINED (1 OF 3): ROLE, EVENT OR ARTIFACT?

Ask one question: is it a who, a when, or a what?

1

Who? It is a person = role

3 roles: Product Owner (what to build, in what order), Scrum Master (keeps the process working), Developers (build it).

2

When? It happens on the calendar = event

5 events: Sprint, Sprint Planning, Daily Scrum, Sprint Review, Retrospective.

3

What? You can open it or show it = artifact

3 artifacts: Product Backlog, Sprint Backlog, Increment.

TermTypeHow you know
Product OwnerRoleA person: ranks the backlog
Daily ScrumEventA 15-min daily meeting
Sprint BacklogArtifactA list you can open
Scrum MasterRoleA person, not the boss
RetrospectiveEventA meeting, end of sprint
IncrementArtifactThe slice you can demo
Common traps

"Sprint Backlog" and "Sprint Review" share a word but differ: one is a list (artifact), one is a meeting (event). The Sprint itself is an event: the box that holds the other events.

intro2se-ch9-project-management.fig / Concept check 5, explained: burndown
CONCEPT CHECK 5, EXPLAINED (2 OF 3): HOW TO READ A BURNDOWN

The y-axis is work left: higher means behind

1

Find today on the x-axis

Day 5 of 10: halfway through the sprint.

2

Read both lines

Ideal = 50% left. Actual = 70% left.

3

Compare

Actual above ideal = more work left than planned = behind. The ideal drops 10% a day, so a 20% gap is about 2 days behind.

4

Act now

Say it at the Daily Scrum: split the stuck task, pair on it, or agree with the Product Owner what to drop.

Common traps

"Higher line = more done": no, the line shows work left. A line that goes up means new work was added mid-sprint. Waiting until day 10 to react is the real failure.

intro2se-ch9-project-management.fig / Risk awareness
IDEA 3: NAME THE RISK BEFORE IT BITES

A risk is something that might go wrong — say it out loud

Risk (it might happen)A response you plan now
The one coder gets sick before the demoPair up; commit daily so work isn't on one laptop.
The hard feature turns out impossibleBuild it first; have a simpler fallback ready.
A teammate goes quietVisible board + daily check-in surfaces it early, not at the end.
Light, not heavy

You don't need a risk register — just name your top one or two and decide what you'd do. Surprises hurt small teams the most.

IDEA 3b: RISK MANAGEMENT (SOMMERVILLE §22.1)

Four steps, three kinds of risk

→

Monitor
re-check each sprint
1

Project risks

Hit the schedule or resources — e.g. a teammate leaves.

2

Product risks

Hit quality or performance — e.g. the hard feature is too slow.

3

Business risks

Hit the wider goal — e.g. no one actually needs it.

Source: Sommerville 9e, §22.1 Risk management — identify → analyse → plan → monitor.

In plain terms

It's weather forecasting for your project: spot the storm coming (identify), judge how bad it'll be (analyse), pack an umbrella or change the route (plan), and keep watching the sky (monitor).

intro2se-ch9-project-management.fig / The retrospective
IDEA 5: HOW TEAMS ACTUALLY IMPROVE

End each sprint with three honest questions

Keep

What went well?

Name it so you do it again on purpose — not by luck.

Drop

What didn't?

Be specific and blameless — the process failed, not the person.

Change

What will we do differently?

Pick one change for next sprint. One real change beats ten wishes.

15 minutes, every cycle. This single habit is what separates a team that improves from one that repeats the same bad week.

intro2se-ch9-project-management.fig / Concept check 7
CONCEPT CHECK 7: A BLAMELESS RETROSPECTIVE

Say it again, about the process, not the person

"Because Ton was slow, the team missed the deadline."

"เพราะต้นทำงานช้า ทีมเลยส่งไม่ทัน" (talk to your neighbour for 1 minute)

One good rewrite

"The search-page task was too big, and nobody saw it was stuck until the last day. Next sprint we'll split tasks smaller and check in every day."

It names the process problem, blames no one, and commits to one change.
